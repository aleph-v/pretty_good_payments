# Sequencing System

The sequencing system determines who can submit blocks and when. It balances the need for reliable block production with permissionless participation through a hybrid epoch-based model.

## The Sequencer Role

Sequencers are the operators who collect transactions, batch them into blobs, and submit blocks to L1. They perform a critical service - without sequencers, no transactions would be processed. In return, they earn yield generated by deposited funds.

Anyone can become a sequencer by staking ETH. This stake serves as a security bond: if a sequencer submits invalid data, challengers can prove the fraud and claim half the stake as a reward. The threat of slashing incentivizes honest behavior.

## Epochs and Submission Windows

Time is divided into fixed-length epochs. Each epoch is split into two periods with different submission rules:

**Closed Period** (first half of each epoch): Only a single designated "priority sequencer" can submit blocks. This guarantees them an uncontested window to submit without gas competition. Priority sequencers rotate through a list, so each gets regular turns.

**Open Period** (second half of each epoch): Any sequencer with sufficient stake can submit blocks. If multiple sequencers want to submit, they compete through normal Ethereum transaction ordering. This ensures the system remains live even if priority sequencers go offline.

This design provides predictability for priority sequencers while maintaining decentralization and censorship resistance through the open period.

## Priority Sequencer Assignment

The contract owner maintains a list of priority sequencers. For any given epoch, the priority sequencer is determined by taking the epoch number modulo the list length. This means priority sequencers take turns in round-robin fashion.

For example, with three priority sequencers A, B, and C:
- Epoch 0, 3, 6, 9... → Sequencer A
- Epoch 1, 4, 7, 10... → Sequencer B
- Epoch 2, 5, 8, 11... → Sequencer C

If a priority sequencer is removed or slashed, the list is compacted using a swap-and-pop pattern to avoid gaps in the rotation.

## Becoming a Sequencer

To become a sequencer, an address must stake at least the required amount of ETH by calling `fund()`. The stake is stored in compressed form (units of 10^14 wei) to fit the entire sequencer status in a single storage slot.

Once funded, the sequencer becomes "active" and can submit blocks during open periods. To also submit during closed periods, the sequencer must be added to the priority list by the contract owner.

A sequencer can add more stake at any time by calling `fund()` again. However, if the sequencer has been challenged (fraud proven), they cannot add more stake - their status is frozen pending resolution.

## Block Submission Process

When a sequencer wants to submit a block, they:

1. **Check permission**: Call `isAllowed()` to verify they can submit in the current period. During closed periods, only the current priority sequencer returns true. During open periods, any active sequencer with sufficient stake returns true.

2. **Gather data**: Collect pending transactions from their mempool and the required deposits from the contract's `perBlockDeposits` mapping.

3. **Build the blob**: Arrange deposits and transactions in the blob memory layout, computing merkle roots as they go.

4. **Submit**: Call `post()` with the block metadata and blob indices. The contract verifies permission, checks deposit counts match, stores the block hash, and tracks blob usage for yield distribution.

The contract enforces that block numbers are sequential - you can only submit block N if block N-1 already exists.

## Blob Usage Tracking

Sequencers earn yield proportional to how much blob space they fill. The system tracks "blob usage" as the total field count used:

- Each deposit uses 1 field (plus overhead for group roots)
- Each transaction uses 15 fields

When a block is submitted, the contract calculates the raw blob usage and records it for the current epoch. Priority sequencers submitting during their closed period receive a 2x multiplier on their usage, incentivizing them to actively use their guaranteed window.

At the end of each epoch, sequencers can claim yield based on their share of total blob usage. A sequencer with 40% of the epoch's blob usage receives 40% of that epoch's yield allocation.

## Exiting the System

Sequencers can exit and reclaim their stake, but not instantly. The exit process has two steps:

**Register Exit**: The sequencer calls `registerExit()` which immediately deactivates them (they can no longer submit blocks) and records the current timestamp. If they were a priority sequencer, they're removed from the rotation.

**Complete Exit**: After the challenge window has elapsed, anyone can call `exit(sequencerAddress)` to send the stake back. The delay ensures that if the sequencer submitted fraud in their final blocks, challengers have time to prove it before the stake disappears.

If a sequencer is successfully challenged after registering exit but before completing it, the exit is blocked and the stake is forfeited to the challenger.

## Slashing Mechanics

When fraud is proven against a sequencer:

1. The sequencer is immediately deactivated
2. The challenger's address and the fraudulent block number are recorded
3. If the sequencer was a priority sequencer, they're removed from the list
4. After the challenge window, the challenger can claim 50% of the stake

Multiple challengers may prove fraud against the same sequencer for different blocks. The recorded "earliest fraud" is the one with the lowest block number - that challenger gets the reward. This incentivizes finding the earliest instance of fraud to maximize rollback.

The remaining 50% of the stake is not returned to anyone - it's effectively burned. This ensures sequencers always lose more than challengers gain, making fraud unprofitable even with collusion.

## Economic Considerations

### Revenue Sources

Sequencers earn through yield distribution. The amount depends on:
- Total value locked (TVL) in the system
- Yield rate of the underlying vaults
- Sequencer's share of blob usage

Priority sequencers have an advantage: their 2x multiplier during closed periods means they can earn more with the same amount of data submitted.

### Costs

Sequencers must pay for:
- **ETH stake**: Capital locked in the contract
- **Block submission gas**: ~60,000-130,000 gas per block
- **Blob gas**: 131,072 blob gas per blob (EIP-4844 pricing)
- **Infrastructure**: Running sequencer and challenger nodes

### Risk

The main risk is slashing. A sequencer who submits invalid data loses half their stake. This could happen due to:
- Software bugs
- Malicious operators
- Responding to invalid user transactions

Running a challenger alongside the sequencer helps catch issues before submission, but the sequencer is ultimately responsible for the validity of their blocks.

## Relationship to Yield

The sequencing system is tightly coupled with yield distribution. The `totalBlobUse` and `sequencerBlobUse` mappings track per-epoch usage, which the YieldRouter queries when sequencers claim rewards.

A sequencer who never submits blocks earns nothing, regardless of their stake. A sequencer who submits empty blocks (deposits only, no transactions) still earns based on deposit blob usage, but less than one processing transactions.

This creates natural pressure for sequencers to actively process transactions rather than simply staking and waiting.
